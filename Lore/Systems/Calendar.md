# Calendar Systems

Two distinct calendar systems exist in this world, reflecting the broader philosophical divide between Senara and Praxis.

---

## Senara — The Natural Calendar

Senara's calendar is observational. Time is not measured by fixed counts or instruments, but by what can be seen and felt. The world tells you when something has changed; you simply notice.

### Seasons

Each season has a natural trigger — an observable event that signals the season is imminent. These triggers are signs, not declarations. The Aelia blooming does not mean spring has begun; it means spring is coming. The official start of each season is declared by the church in Vatican, who read the natural signs and make a formal announcement at the new moon preceding the change. Seasons always begin on a new moon.

| Season | Trigger |
|--------|---------|
| **Spring** | The bloom of the Aelia flower — a small white-gold bloom that appears after the last frost, named for the Saintess of Dawn |
| **Summer** | The migration of the solarn birds, whose arrival marks the height of warmth |
| **Autumn** | The first red-turn of the karath trees, whose leaves shift before any chill is felt |
| **Winter** | The freezing of the Mira streams — shallow, fast-moving waterways that are the last to ice |

The church's declaration is made at the new moon before the season turns, giving exactly one full moon of notice. Most people already expect roughly when the change will come — the declaration is usually a confirmation, not a surprise. The exception is when signs are ambiguous or a season lingers unexpectedly long, in which case the church may hold the declaration another moon.

### Planning Events

Because seasons always start on a new moon and the church announces one moon in advance, events anchored to a season's start can be planned with reasonable notice. "The first day of spring" is always a new moon, always church-declared, and always known at least a month ahead. Deeper into a season, dates shift cycle to cycle but remain predictable enough once the season has begun.

### Months

Months are tracked by the moon. One full lunar cycle is one month. Senarans count moons rather than name them — "three moons ago" is a natural expression. The moon is visible and universal; no record-keeping is required.

A month begins with the new moon — when the moon is absent from the sky. From nothing, it grows, peaks at full, fades, and disappears again. That completed cycle is one month. The four phases divide the month into rough quarters and serve as the primary reference points for dating events within a month.

| Phase    | Moon state                        |
|----------|-----------------------------------|
| **New**  | Moon is dark; the month begins    |
| **Wax**  | Moon is growing bright            |
| **Full** | Moon at peak; the brightest night |
| **Wane** | Moon is fading to dark            |

### Moons of a Season

Because seasons always start and end on a new moon, every moon within a season is a complete lunar cycle — no partial moons, no ambiguity about which season a moon belongs to. With thirteen moons in a year and four seasons, three seasons will have three moons and one will have four. This is not rare — it happens every year, just rotating between seasons unpredictably.

Moons are numbered from the season's first new moon. Spring's first moon, spring's second moon, and so on. The count is simply how many new moons have occurred since the season began.

Each quarter of the moon serves as a rough unit of time — roughly a week. Senarans call this a **tide**. "In two tides" means roughly a fortnight. No counting of days within a tide is required; the moon's appearance tells you where you are. Praxis uses the word **phase** for the equivalent unit.

Combined with a phase, this gives a precise enough reference that two people across the country can identify the same window without any written calendar. Formally, the full phrase includes "moon" and the complete phase name. Informally, "moon" is dropped and waxing/waning are shortened to wax and wane:

| Formal                     | Informal             |
|----------------------------|----------------------|
| Spring's first new moon    | Spring's first new   |
| Spring's first waxing moon | Spring's first wax   |
| Summer's second full moon  | Summer's second full |
| Autumn's third waning moon | Autumn's third wane  |

For a single day, a count can be added: *"three days into summer's first wax."*

### Days

Days are not divided by the clock. Each period is a specific, observable state of the sun and sky — not a label for a rough stretch of time. These words carry precise natural meaning, and using them loosely would simply be inaccurate.

Four moments mark transitions between periods:

| Moment   | Description             |
|----------|-------------------------|
| **Dawn** | The sun rises           |
| **Sola** | The sun is at its peak  |
| **Dusk** | The sun sets            |
| **Luna** | The moon is at its peak |

The eight periods between them:

| Period            | Clock Approx. | Description                                           |
|-------------------|---------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
| **Early morning** | ~4am – 8am    | The dark before dawn; bridges night and day           |
| **Morning**       | ~7am – 11am   | Sun fully risen and climbing; the working hours begin |
| **Midday**        | ~10am – 2pm   | The sun at or near its peak; the heat of the day      |
| **Aftersola**     | ~1pm – 5pm    | Sun past its peak and descending                      |
| **Evening**       | ~4pm – 8pm    | The sun setting; the sky is colored                   |
| **Night**         | ~7pm – 11pm   | Full dark, but the night is young                     |
| **Midnight**      | ~10pm – 2am   | The moon at its peak; the mirror of midday            |
| **Afterluna**     | ~1am – 5am    | The moon past its peak; stillness, few are about      |

This is distinct from how the words function in casual Praxian speech. In Praxis, clock time governs all real scheduling, so "morning" or "evening" in conversation may refer loosely to any part of that general stretch. The word is approximate flavor, not a specific condition. In Senara, it is the condition.

Because these periods track the actual sun and moon, they shift with the seasons. The names stay the same — only the clock equivalent drifts.

Some Senarans, particularly merchants or city-dwellers, keep mechanical clocks — but only to *approximate* the day's position, not to govern it. Telling someone to meet you "around midday" is precise enough. No one schedules the third hour of the afternoon.

### Years

Senara has no year concept. The seasons cycle continuously — winter does not end a year, spring does not begin one. A "year" is just a casual word for one full seasonal lap, useful for rough estimation but not a formal unit. There is no start, no end, no count.

Notable periods of history are recorded not as numbered years but as named eras — spans of time defined by what made them significant. These do not map cleanly to equal units. Some span a single season, others stretch across many. Some overlap in cultural memory. "The long winters" might refer to three consecutive harsh seasons felt as one unbroken stretch. "The bloom years" might describe a generation of prosperity that different people remember as starting and ending at different points.

A Praxian historian attempting to convert Senaran history into numbered years will find it structurally resistant — not because Senarans are imprecise, but because their history is not organized as a sequence of equal intervals. It is a collection of things that happened, remembered by what they were, not when.

## Praxis — The Revolutionary Calendar

Following the civil war and the establishment of the new government, Praxis formalized a standardized calendar as part of its modernization effort. Consistency, accountability, and record-keeping were founding principles of the new state.

### Year Count

Years are numbered from the year of the Revolution — Year 1 PR (Post-Revolution). The story begins in **Year 538 PR**.

### Months and Weeks

Praxis did not discard the lunar intuition — they standardized it. A Senaran month is roughly one lunar cycle; a Praxian month is fixed at exactly 28 days, closely approximating the moon. A Senaran phase is roughly a quarter of a moon; a Praxian week is fixed at exactly 7 days, approximating a phase. Four weeks per month, thirteen months per year.

13 months × 28 days = 364 days, plus one intercalary day — **Revolution Day** — which belongs to no month and falls at the year's end. Every few years a second intercalary day is added to account for the fractional orbital year.

Months and days of the week have names, to be defined. Note: 7-day weeks and 13-month years are both prime numbers, making the calendar arithmetically inconvenient — quarters, halves, and other divisions don't work cleanly. A more functional structure is to be revisited.

### Time of Day

Praxis uses clock time. Hours are fixed and numbered, and punctuality is a civic virtue. Public clocks exist in most city centers. Appointments, court times, and military movements are all scheduled to the hour.

### Conversion Note

Because Senara does not number years, aligning Senaran historical events to the Praxian calendar requires estimation. Scholars typically anchor events to well-documented natural occurrences — a long winter, a drought year — and work outward from there. Exact synchronization is often impossible and considered a Praxian concern more than a Senaran one.
